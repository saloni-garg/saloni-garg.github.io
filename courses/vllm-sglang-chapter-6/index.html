Chapter map

Four questions, in the order production asks them

01 · Mechanics

What actually changes?

Weights, activations, and KV cache are three separate quantization decisions. Conflating them is how teams benchmark one thing and deploy another.

02 · Kernels

Does the fast path actually run?

A format is only as fast as its kernel. Silent fallback to a dequantize-then-BF16 path can make INT4 slower than BF16 — and nothing in the latency graph tells you why.

03 · Quality

Where does the loss concentrate?

Not in average perplexity. In long-context retrieval, multi-hop grounding, and the tail of the doc QA golden set — exactly where the paying tenant lives.

04 · Economics

What is it worth?

Goodput at SLO and $/1M tokens per cell, from the Chapter 5 harness. Quality delta without a price is trivia; price without quality is a trap.

Mechanics

“We quantized the model” is three decisions, not one

Weights

W

Stored precision of the parameters. Decided offline, shipped in the checkpoint. Drives memory footprint and decode bandwidth.

+
Activations

A

Precision of the tensors flowing between layers, and of the GEMM inputs. Decides whether tensor cores run in the low-precision path at all.

+
KV cache

KV

Precision of stored keys and values. Independent of W and A in both engines — and, per Chapter 3, it is your concurrency ceiling.

Weight-only (W8A16, W4A16)

Weights stored small, dequantized to BF16 before the GEMM. Saves memory and bandwidth; compute stays BF16. AWQ-INT4 lives here.

Weight + activation (W8A8)

Both GEMM operands in FP8. Unlocks FP8 tensor cores on H100 — the only variant that speeds up prefill compute, not just decode bandwidth.

KV quantization

Orthogonal flag in both engines (kv_cache_dtype in vLLM, --kv-cache-dtype fp8_e4m3 in SGLang). Can be combined with any W/A choice.

When a vendor says “FP8 serving,” ask which of the three they mean. The three answers have different speed, quality, and failure profiles.

FP8 · anatomy

Two FP8s: E4M3 for precision, E5M2 for range

E4M3 — 4 exponent, 3 mantissa

SEEEEMMM
  • Max normal value: 448. Relative step near 1.0: 2−3 = 12.5% granularity per binade, finer than E5M2.
  • The default for weights, activations, and KV in both engines on Hopper.
  • No infinity encoding; NaN takes the top pattern. Out-of-range values saturate or corrupt — scaling is not optional.

E5M2 — 5 exponent, 2 mantissa

SEEEEEMM
  • Max normal value: 57,344 — same exponent range shape as BF16, coarser mantissa.
  • Historically used for gradients in training, where range beats precision.
  • Rarely the right inference choice: if your tensors need E5M2 range at inference, your scaling strategy is the real bug.

Mantissa bits are where quantization error lives. Going BF16 (7 mantissa bits) → FP8 E4M3 (3) quadruples the rounding step; E5M2 (2) doubles it again. Everything in the next three slides is about keeping the values you care about away from that rounding error.

FP8 · scaling

Scaling is the whole game

FP8 stores value ≈ fp8_code × scale. The scale decides which slice of the real line gets the 256 available codes. Granularity of the scale is the main quality knob in FP8 serving:

Coarsest

Per-tensor

One scale for an entire weight matrix or activation tensor. One outlier channel stretches the range and pushes everything else toward zero codes. Cheapest, and the first suspect when quality drops.

Middle

Per-channel / per-token

A scale per output channel (weights) or per token row (activations). The common production default for W8A8 checkpoints: outliers are quarantined to their own scale.

Finest

Block-wise (128×128)

A scale per tile, DeepSeek-style. Best error profile, more scale traffic in the kernel, and engine support is the newest — verify it runs before you plan around it.

Static (calibrated) scales

Activation scales measured once on calibration data and frozen. Deterministic, no runtime cost — and silently wrong when production traffic drifts away from the calibration distribution.

Dynamic scales

Scale computed from the live tensor's amax each step. Tracks drift, costs a reduction pass per tensor. Both engines default to dynamic activation scaling for FP8 unless the checkpoint ships static scales.

FP8 · payoff on H100

Where FP8 actually pays — and where it cannot

Decode: bandwidth-bound → weights halve

Chapter 4's roofline: a decode step reads all weights once. At TP4, BF16 reads 35 GB per GPU per step; FP8 reads 17.5 GB.

BF16 step floor
10.4 ms
FP8 step floor
5.2 ms

Floor = weight bytes ÷ 3.35 TB/s HBM. Real steps sit above the floor; the gap is KV reads, attention, and kernel efficiency.

Prefill: compute-bound → only W8A8 helps

  • H100 dense FP8 tensor-core peak is ≈ 2× BF16 (1,979 vs 989 TFLOPS, sparsity excluded).
  • Weight-only FP8 (W8A16) dequantizes before the GEMM: prefill runs at BF16 speed. The chat tenant feels nothing at TTFT.
  • W8A8 runs the GEMM in FP8 end to end: this is the variant that cuts the 18K-token doc prefill, and the one with activation-outlier exposure.
Decision shorthand: decode-heavy, latency-bound → any FP8 variant helps. Prefill-heavy → only W8A8 helps, and it is the variant that needs the quality gate. Our two-tenant mix has one of each, which is why this chapter measures both.

KV cache · FP8

FP8 KV: Chapter 3's ceiling, doubled

0.3125 MiB/tok

BF16 KV. 80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 head-dim × 2 (K and V) × 2 bytes = 327,680 B per token.

0.15625 MiB/tok

FP8 KV. Same geometry, 1 byte per element: 163,840 B per token. Exactly half, because nothing else in the formula changes.

≈ 2×

Concurrency. On the Chapter 3 budget, the fleet-wide token pool goes from ≈ 391K to ≈ 782K tokens — before weight savings free even more memory.

The fine print: K is not V

Keys pass through RoPE and are compared by dot product, so K quantization error rotates attention logits directly. Several production reports — and our golden set in slide 15 — show K is the more sensitive half. If an engine offers per-tensor KV scales only, expect the long-context tail to pay first.

Interaction with prefix caching

FP8 KV halves the cost of keeping cached prefixes, which lengthens the economically sensible retention window from Chapter 3. But cached FP8 blocks are written once at quantized precision — a block shared by 40 sessions carries one quantization error, amplified by reuse.

Kernels · the trap

The silent fallback: when “INT4” runs slower than BF16

A quantized checkpoint does not guarantee a quantized kernel. Shape, group size, activation dtype, and engine version all gate the fast path. The failure mode is quiet: correct outputs, wrong speed.

AWQ · the idea

AWQ: protect the weights the activations care about

Activation-aware Weight Quantization starts from an empirical fact: weight importance is not uniform, and it is predictable from activation statistics, not from the weights alone.

≈ 1%

of weight channels carry disproportionately large activations — the salient channels. Their quantization error dominates output error.

The trick: equivalent scaling

Scale a salient weight channel up by s before quantizing, and scale the corresponding activation channel down by s. The product is mathematically unchanged; the weight now sits in a finer region of the INT4 grid.

Choosing s

s is searched per layer on calibration data to minimize output error, typically from the channel's average activation magnitude raised to a tuned exponent. No retraining, no gradients through the model — a calibration pass, then search.

Why this matters for serving: AWQ's protection is baked in at quantization time from calibration activations. Change the traffic, and the channels that matter may change with it. That dependency is slide 12.

AWQ · mechanics

Inside an AWQ checkpoint: groups, scales, packing

Group-wise INT4

  • Weights quantized in groups of 128 along the input dimension; each group gets its own BF16 scale and a packed zero-point.
  • Error is bounded per group: a large weight inflates only its group's scale, not the whole row's.
  • Group 64 halves the metadata efficiency loss but shrinks error further; group 256 does the opposite. 128 is the ecosystem default and the best-supported kernel shape.

Packing and the Marlin kernel

  • Two INT4 values pack into one byte; the checkpoint stores weights pre-shuffled into the layout the Marlin kernel wants, so dequantization fuses into the GEMM's inner loop.
  • Marlin's trick: convert INT4 → FP16 in-register, overlapped with the tensor-core math, so W4A16 decode approaches the bandwidth floor instead of paying a separate dequant pass.
  • That is why slide 9's check matters: without Marlin (or SGLang's equivalent AWQ kernel), AWQ is just extra work wrapped around a BF16 GEMM.

AWQ · calibration dependence

Calibration data is a production dependency

AWQ (and static-scale FP8) freeze decisions from a calibration corpus. The corpus is small — typically 128–512 sequences — which makes which sequences a first-order quality variable.

Quality · distribution of harm

Perplexity barely moves. Retrieval falls over.

Why the average lies

Perplexity averages over mostly easy, high-frequency tokens. Quantization error is not uniform: it concentrates where activations are large, contexts are long, and the answer depends on one span among thousands of tokens.

  • Short chat: robust. Redundant signal, short KV, errors wash out.
  • Needle retrieval at 18K: the query must attend to one passage; K/V rounding noise competes with a single weak signal.
  • Multi-hop grounding: two retrievals chained — errors compound multiplicatively, not additively.

Quality · method

The golden sets: scored per tenant, sliced by failure mode

Chat tenant
  • 400 prompts drawn from production-shaped chat: short, multi-turn, system prompt shared.
  • Scored: exact-match on structured answers, plus rubric-graded open answers with the grader fixed across cells.
  • Slices: single-turn vs turn ≥ 4 (KV length grows inside a “short” tenant too).
Doc QA tenant
  • 300 questions over 18K-token documents: extractive, needle-at-depth (25% / 50% / 90% of context), and two-hop.
  • Scored: span F1 for extractive, exact retrieval for needles, answer correctness for hops.
  • Every slice reported separately. A cell ships only if every slice clears its floor — no averaging away the tail.

Fixed across cells

Prompts, sampling (temperature 0), max tokens, grader version, and engine flags other than precision.

Reported per cell

Score per slice, delta vs the same engine's BF16 cell, and a paired bootstrap interval on the delta — differences under ~0.5 pts on 700 items are noise until proven otherwise.

The gate

Pre-registered floors: chat −0.5 pts, doc QA overall −1.0 pt, any single slice −2.0 pts. A cell that fails the gate is out of the economics table, whatever it costs.

Decision

Quantization is a measurement, not a setting

1 · Separate the three decisions

Weights, activations, KV. Each has its own speed mechanism, quality risk, and engine flag. Never accept a single “quantized: yes” as a deployment description.

2 · Gate on the tail, price on goodput

Ship decisions come from worst-slice quality and at-SLO economics. Averages — perplexity, peak throughput — are how bad configurations get promoted.

3 · Own your calibration

The checkpoint is a bet on a data distribution. Make the bet yourself, version it, and re-make it when the traffic changes.

For this workload, the answer

FP8 W8A8 with FP8 KV on either engine: worst slice −1.4 pts, 1.61× BF16 goodput, $0.22–0.23 per 1M tokens. AWQ-INT4 stays in the toolbox for memory-bound deployments — with in-house calibration, or not at all.
